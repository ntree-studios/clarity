# Writing Technique: Paragraphs, Coherence, Cohesion, Information Flow, and Staying on Subject

Scope: established principles of expository writing at sentence-to-paragraph and paragraph-to-document level, stated as testable prescriptions, with notes on what a spaCy-based linter ("clarity") could observe. Evidence strength is tagged as **[opinion]** (style-guide prescription), **[theory]** (linguistic or computational model), or **[empirical]** (comprehension or essay-scoring data).

Source-access caveat: Gopen & Swan (1990), Strunk (1918), Orwell (1946), McNamara/Crossley/McCarthy (2010), the Purdue OWL and the GOV.UK guidance were read in full text. Williams' *Style*, Pinker's *Sense of Style*, Halliday & Hasan, Minto, Barzilay & Lapata and Centering Theory are known here only through secondary summaries, abstracts and reviews. Treat those findings as faithful to the gist but not verbatim.

## 1. Paragraph unity and topic sentences

### Takeaway
All the authorities agree that each paragraph should cover one topic, with a topic sentence at or near its start. Strunk adds that the final sentence should restate or extend the topic sentence and never end on a digression. None of the major guides sets a numeric paragraph length except GOV.UK, which caps paragraphs at 5 sentences. Purdue warns in the opposite direction, against paragraphs of only 2–3 sentences. All of this is opinion. No comprehension study cited here tests topic-sentence placement directly.

### Cited Findings
- Strunk, rule: "Make the paragraph the unit of composition: one paragraph to each topic." **[opinion]** — [Strunk, Elements of Style (1918), Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm)
- Strunk, rule: "As a rule, begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end it in conformity with the beginning." The most useful paragraph is one where "the topic sentence comes at or near the beginning". The "final sentence either emphasizes the thought of the topic sentence or states some important consequence". "Ending with a digression, or with an unimportant detail, is particularly to be avoided." **[opinion]** — [Strunk](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm)
- Strunk on length: "As a rule, single sentences should not be written or printed as paragraphs. An exception may be made of sentences of transition." "The extent of subdivision will vary with the length of the composition." He gives no numeric limit. — [Strunk](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm)
- Purdue OWL lists four paragraph traits: unity, coherence, a topic sentence, and adequate development. Unity: "The entire paragraph should concern itself with a single focus." Purdue says not to begin with one point and drift into another. **[opinion]** — [Purdue OWL, Paragraphs and Paragraphing](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html)
- Purdue on topic sentences: they can appear anywhere, but writers should "put your topic sentence near the beginning of the paragraph" (this especially helps less experienced writers). Whatever the placement, the writer should be able to summarise the paragraph's topic. — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html)
- Purdue on development and length: "writers should be wary of paragraphs that only have two or three sentences". "New ideas should always start in new paragraphs". Break a paragraph when "the paragraph becomes too long or the material is complex". Purdue gives no numeric maximum. — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html)
- GOV.UK: "Paragraphs should have no more than 5 sentences each." **[opinion, web/plain-language context]** — [GOV.UK Writing guidelines: clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)
- Williams & Bizup: unity means "focusing it on one subject or idea, signaled by a topic sentence that announces the paragraph's main point" (p. 67, as quoted). A reader judges a paragraph coherent if they can find a sentence that states its POINT. — [Writing Commons (quoting Williams & Bizup)](https://writingcommons.org/section/style/); [sobrief summary of Style](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/style-2)
- Daneš's "hypertheme" (a theme from which the themes of several sentences derive) was reworked by J.R. Martin into a paragraph-level "hyper-Theme", which is in effect a topic sentence. One commentator argues that Martin's version just renames the topic sentence. — [discourse-semantics blog on Martin 1992](https://discourse-semantics.blogspot.com/2016_02_20_archive.html)

### Inferences (machine-observable correlates)
- **Topic-sentence proxy:** call sentence 1 the candidate topic sentence. Compute lemma overlap (content nouns, verbs, noun-chunk heads) between S1 and each later sentence S2..Sn. If S1 shares nothing with most later sentences while some later sentence Sk overlaps more with the rest, the paragraph probably has a buried or missing topic sentence.
- **Unity/drift proxy:** if the noun-chunk heads in the second half of a paragraph barely overlap with those in the first half (Jaccard below a threshold), the paragraph is starting one topic and drifting into another. This is Purdue's description of a unity failure.
- **Strunk's ending rule:** the last sentence should overlap lexically with S1 or carry a consequence connective ("so", "therefore", "as a result", "this means"). Flag a last sentence that introduces new entities found nowhere else in the paragraph. That is the "ending with a digression" failure.
- **Length rules:** count sentences per paragraph. Flag paragraphs over 5 sentences (GOV.UK) or over a configurable word count. Flag single-sentence paragraphs (Strunk) unless they are short and contain a transitional connective. Configuration should state that Purdue and GOV.UK pull in opposite directions on length.

### Gaps
- No empirical comprehension study on topic-sentence placement was found in this pass. Braddock's 1974 finding that professional essays often lack explicit topic sentences is often cited against the rule, but I did not retrieve it.
- No numeric paragraph-length guidance was found from Microsoft, Google or The Economist.

## 2. Coherence and information flow (Williams, Gopen & Swan, Pinker, Halliday & Hasan, Daneš, Centering)

### Takeaway
Williams, Gopen & Swan, Daneš and Centering Theory converge on one prescription. The sentence's opening (its topic or subject position) should hold a familiar, backward-linking entity, usually the "character" whose story it is. That entity should stay stable across a paragraph (a topic string, constant theme, or Centering CONTINUE). New, emphasised information goes in the stress position at the end. The verb should follow its subject closely and should carry the action, not a nominalization. These claims are mostly expert opinion and theory, with partial corpus support from Centering and entity-grid work.

### Cited Findings
**Gopen & Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (American Scientist, 1990)** **[opinion, grounded in reader-expectation argument]**
- Their summary list of seven reader-expectation principles: "1. Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb. 2. Place in the stress position the 'new information' you want the reader to emphasize. 3. Place the person or thing whose 'story' a sentence is telling at the beginning of the sentence, in the topic position. 4. Place appropriate 'old information' (material already stated in the discourse) in the topic position for linkage backward and contextualization forward. 5. Articulate the action of every clause or sentence in its verb. 6. In general, provide context for your reader before asking that reader to consider anything new. 7. In general, try to ensure that the relative emphases of the substance coincide with the relative expectations for emphasis raised by the structure." — [Gopen & Swan 1990 (PDF)](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf)
- Subject–verb separation: in their first example, the subject is separated from its verb by 23 words. "Readers expect a grammatical subject to be followed immediately by the verb. Anything of length that intervenes between subject and verb is read as an interruption, and therefore as something of lesser importance." — [Gopen & Swan](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf)
- "Every unit of discourse, no matter the size, should serve a single function or make a single point." — [Gopen & Swan](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf)
- Stress position: "readers naturally emphasize the material that arrives at the end of a sentence". "The stress position coincides with the moment of syntactic closure." A colon or semicolon creates a secondary stress position. — [Gopen & Swan](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf)
- Sentence length redefined: "A sentence is too long when it has more viable candidates for stress positions than there are stress positions available". They reject word-count limits and allow "100-word sentences that flow effortlessly". — [Gopen & Swan](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf)
- Topic position: "Readers expect a unit of discourse to be a story about whoever shows up first." In their earthquake example, the recurring concept ("recurrence intervals") "rarely appears at the beginning of sentences," so the focus seems to shift. — [Gopen & Swan](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf)
- Caveat against over-simplification: "Many people … oversimplify it to the following rule: 'Put the old information in the topic position and the new information in the stress position.' No such rule is possible." The principle is: "Put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize." Also: "None of these reader-expectation principles should be considered 'rules.'" — [Gopen & Swan](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf)
- Logical gaps: "When old information does not appear at all in a sentence … readers are left to construct the logical linkage by themselves." This happens when connections are clear to the writer, which is a curse-of-knowledge effect. — [Gopen & Swan](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf)
- Strunk anticipated two of these principles. "Keep related words together": subject and verb should not be "separated by a phrase or clause that can be transferred to the beginning". "The relative pronoun should come, as a rule, immediately after its antecedent". "Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end": "The other prominent position in the sentence is the beginning". He says the principle applies to sentences within paragraphs and paragraphs within a composition. — [Strunk](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm)

**Joseph Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*** **[opinion; secondary sources]**
- Characters as subjects, actions as verbs: prose feels clear when "(1) the subjects of the sentences name the cast of characters, and (2) the verbs that go with those subjects name the crucial actions those characters are part of." — [sobrief summary](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/style-2); [kspeicherwrite blog on "Characters" lesson](https://kspeicherwrite.blogspot.com/2006/03/in-characters-fourth-lesson-of-style.html)
- Nominalizations (abstract nouns made from verbs or adjectives) are the "primary culprits" in hiding actions and characters. — [sobrief](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/style-2)
- Topic strings: the topic, usually the grammatical subject, should carry familiar information. Random topic shifts disorient readers. "Thematic strings" (networks of related words) reinforce the core ideas. — [sobrief](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/style-2)
- Williams treats cohesion as local, from "beginning a sentence with familiar points and words and ending with new or lengthy material" (p. 81). He treats coherence as "a sense of the whole" (p. 83). — [Gospel Coalition review of Style](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/blogs/justin-taylor/style-some-notes-on-clarity-and-grace/)
- Global coherence: a reader finds a paragraph coherent when "she can read a sentence that specifically articulates its POINT". Williams' issue + point structure puts the point at the end of a short opening "issue" segment. — [sobrief](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/style-2); [Writing Commons](https://writingcommons.org/section/style/)

**Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014), ch. 5 "Arcs of Coherence"** **[opinion informed by psycholinguistics; secondary sources]**
- The chapter covers how to make sure readers "grasp the topic, get the point, keep track of the players, and see how one idea follows from another". Pinker argues that the hunger for coherence drives comprehension: readers need to see a connection between any two consecutive sentences. — [chapter 5 excerpt](https://zucc-books.pages.dev/the_sense_of_style/chapter_5_arcs_of_coherence.html); [Geoff Hart review](https://geoff-hart.com/articles/2016/sense-of-style.html)
- Practical advice in summaries: state the topic up front, use paragraph breaks, use the right number of connectives. — [Austin Hoover summary](https://austin-hoover.github.io/blog/posts/2024-11-10_the-sense-of-style/index.html)
- Curse of knowledge: "the difficulty in imagining what it is like for someone else not to know something that you know". It produces cryptic, abstruse prose with unexplained jargon and skipped steps. — [Geoff Hart review](https://geoff-hart.com/articles/2016/sense-of-style.html); [ch. 5 excerpt](https://zucc-books.pages.dev/the_sense_of_style/chapter_5_arcs_of_coherence.html)

**Daneš thematic progression (1974)** **[theory]**
- Linear progression: the rheme of one sentence becomes the theme of the next. Constant progression: the same theme repeats across sentences. Derived progression: several themes derive from one inferred hypertheme. Daneš also describes a split-rheme pattern, and the types combine. — [arXiv 2010.07440 (Daneš overview)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.07440)
- Genre differences: constant progression is reported as more frequent in English argumentative texts. Legislative texts are mainly constant-linear. — [ae.fl.kpi.ua genre study](https://ae.fl.kpi.ua/article/view/142658/172250)

**Centering Theory (Grosz, Joshi & Weinstein 1995)** **[theory with corpus support]**
- Transitions between utterances are classified by whether the backward-looking center (Cb) stays the same and whether Cb equals the preferred center (Cp, roughly the highest-ranked entity, usually the subject). The four types are CONTINUE, RETAIN, SMOOTH-SHIFT and ROUGH-SHIFT. Rule 2: "CONTINUE is preferred to RETAIN is preferred to SMOOTH-SHIFT is preferred to ROUGH-SHIFT." Rule 1: if any entity of the previous utterance is pronominalized, the Cb should be too. — [Edinburgh pragmatics notes on reference](https://lel.ed.ac.uk/~hrohde/plc/reference-2syntax.html); [Kibble 2001, Computational Linguistics](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/J01-4007.pdf)
- The Rule 2 ordering is contested, and Kibble (2001) proposes reformulating it. — [Kibble 2001](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/J01-4007.pdf)

### Inferences (machine-observable correlates)
- **Topic string / constant theme (Williams, Daneš constant, Centering CONTINUE):** for each sentence, take the lemma of the `nsubj`/`nsubjpass` head (resolve pronouns to their antecedent where possible, or treat a pronoun as continuing the previous subject). Compute the share of sentences whose subject lemma also appears as subject, or in any role, in the previous sentence or in S1. A testable rule: "the topic sentence's subject (or its pronoun or synonym) recurs as subject in at least ~50% of the paragraph's sentences". The threshold is a heuristic, not from any source.
- **Old-before-new (Gopen & Swan 4, Williams cohesion, Daneš linear):** for sentence Si, test whether its first noun chunk (or subject) shares a lemma with any noun chunk of Si-1. Score the paragraph on how many sentences link backward this way. Linear progression can be detected as overlap between Si's subject and the *last* noun chunk (stress position) of Si-1.
- **Missing linkage (Gopen & Swan "logical gaps"):** flag a sentence that shares no content lemma, pronoun or demonstrative ("this", "these", "such") with the previous sentence and has no connective.
- **Subject–verb distance (Gopen & Swan 1, Strunk):** token distance between the `nsubj` head and its governing verb, excluding the subject's own pre-modifiers if desired. Gopen & Swan's bad example had 23 intervening words. A threshold such as more than 8–10 tokens of intervening material (relative clauses, appositives, parentheticals) is a reasonable heuristic. Coh-Metrix's related index is "words before main verb" (see §6).
- **Action in the verb (Gopen & Swan 5, Williams):** flag sentences whose main verb is a light or empty verb (be, have, make, give, take, perform, conduct, provide, occur) with a nominalization (suffixes -tion, -ment, -ance/-ence, -ity, -al, -ness, -ure) as its subject or object, for example "made a decision", "the implementation of X occurred". Count nominalizations per sentence. The heuristic is a dependency pattern plus a suffix and wordnet derivation check.
- **Characters as subjects:** compute the proportion of subjects that are persons, organisations, pronouns or concrete nouns versus abstract nominalizations (via NER labels, pronoun POS, and the suffix heuristic).
- **Stress position:** the final noun chunk of a sentence should be new to the paragraph, or at least not a repeated pronoun or filler. Flag sentences ending in a pronoun, a prepositional "tail" with no new content ("in this regard", "as well", "at this time"), or a hedge. Flag ones where the most novel noun chunk (not seen in prior sentences) sits mid-sentence while the end is old information.
- **Centering transitions:** approximate Cp as the subject, Cb as the highest-ranked entity of Si-1 that is realised in Si, and compute the transition mix. A high ROUGH-SHIFT ratio signals choppy focus. This is a cruder variant of the entity grid (§6).
- **Single function per unit (Gopen & Swan):** at sentence level, more than two independent clauses each introducing new entities. At paragraph level, see the unity proxies in §1.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Halliday & Hasan (1976) *Cohesion in English*. Standard background knowledge, not verified in this session, lists five cohesive-tie types: reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction and lexical cohesion (reiteration, collocation). Each maps onto a spaCy observable: pronouns/determiners, "one/do so", verb ellipsis, discourse connectives, and lemma or synonym repetition. Cite the primary source before relying on this.
- The exact wording of Pinker's guidance on coherence relations (resemblance, contiguity, cause-effect, after Kehler/Hume) was not retrieved.
- No controlled study was found that tests Gopen & Swan's topic/stress positions on comprehension. The principles rest on psycholinguistic givenness research (given-new contract), which I did not retrieve.

## 3. Staying on subject: digression, headings and answer-first structures

### Takeaway
The guides define staying on subject positively: one point per unit (Gopen & Swan, Strunk, Purdue), a stated point (Williams), and a governing thought on top (Minto). Digression is defined as a sentence or paragraph that does not serve that point. Answer-first structures (BLUF, Minto, Microsoft's "get to the point fast", GOV.UK) prescribe putting the conclusion or action in the first sentence or paragraph. All of this is opinion. None of the guides offers an operational test for digression.

### Cited Findings
- Strunk: "Ending with a digression, or with an unimportant detail, is particularly to be avoided." — [Strunk](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm)
- Gopen & Swan, in revising, "discarded material for which we could not discern significant connections". This is an implicit digression test: no connection to surrounding material means cut it. — [Gopen & Swan](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf)
- Purdue: "New ideas should always start in new paragraphs". Unity fails when a paragraph starts with one focus and moves to another. — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html)
- Minto Pyramid Principle: a single governing thought at the top, with supporting arguments grouped below it, each group summarising the ideas beneath it. "Vertical logic" links the governing thought to its supports. "Horizontal logic" relates sibling points, which should be MECE (mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive). The introduction uses Situation–Complication–Question, then the Answer. — [untools: Minto Pyramid](https://untools.co/minto-pyramid/); [Monash: business paper using the Minto approach](https://www.monash.edu/student-academic-success/excel-at-writing/how-to-write/business-paper-using-the-minto-approach)
- BLUF: Army correspondence "lets the reader know the purpose of the correspondence in the first sentence or paragraph" (attributed to AR 25-50 by a vendor blog, so verify against the regulation). — [AirOps on BLUF](https://www.airops.com/blog/bottom-line-up-front-bluf)
- Microsoft: "Start with the key takeaway. Put the most important thing in the most noticeable spot." "Get to the point fast" is one of its top-10 tips. Summaries add "front-load keywords for scanning". — [Microsoft brand voice page](https://learn.microsoft.com/fr-fr/style-guide/brand-voice-above-all-simple-human); [summary of MS top-10 tips](https://vickyhella.github.io/docs/writing-styles/ms-style-guide)
- Google: "Don't pre-announce anything" in documentation, meaning don't describe what the text will say before saying it. — [Google developer documentation style guide: highlights](https://developers.google.com/style/highlights)
- Pinker warns that too much signposting can bore and confuse a reader. Signposting that announces structure is a form of off-subject metadiscourse when overdone. Williams likewise warns against excessive metadiscourse. — [Journal of Academic Writing review / summaries via Pinker search](https://core.ac.uk/download/229873584.pdf); [sobrief on Williams](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/style-2)

### Inferences (machine-observable correlates)
- **Heading–body relevance:** lemma overlap (or vector similarity) between a heading's content words and the section's first paragraph, especially its first sentence, should be high. Flag sections whose first sentence shares no content lemma with the heading. That suggests the answer or topic is not up front.
- **Answer-first:** flag documents or sections whose first sentence is pure metadiscourse or pre-announcement ("In this section we will discuss…", "This document describes…", "Let's explore…"). Flag those whose first sentence is generic context with no entity overlap with the heading or title.
- **Digression candidate:** a sentence whose content lemmas overlap with neither the paragraph's topic sentence, its neighbours (Si-1, Si+1) nor the section heading. A whole paragraph that overlaps with neither its heading nor the paragraphs on either side is a document-level digression candidate. Low "global cohesion" is the empirical correlate (see §6).
- **MECE/parallelism:** sibling headings, or list items under one heading, should share a grammatical form (all noun phrases or all imperatives, as in Strunk's "Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form"). This can be checked via the POS of each item's first token.

### Gaps
- No authoritative operational definition of "digression" was found. All the tests above are inferences.
- I did not verify the primary text of AR 25-50 or Minto's book. Minto's publication date varies across secondary sources.
- No empirical study was found showing answer-first structure improves comprehension or speed in expository text. Such studies likely exist in the technical-communication literature but were not retrieved.

## 4. Concision and specificity: checkable rules in style guides

### Takeaway
Orwell, Strunk, GOV.UK, Google and Microsoft converge on a set of checkable rules. Prefer the active voice, use short common words over long or Latinate ones, cut needless words, prefer concrete and specific language, address the reader as "you", avoid nominalizations (-ion/-ment), avoid stale figures of speech and jargon, and keep sentences short. The only hard number found is GOV.UK's 25-word sentence limit. Gopen & Swan explicitly reject word-count limits. These are all opinion-based, though GOV.UK cites user-preference data.

### Cited Findings
- Orwell's six rules: "1. Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print. 2. Never use a long word where a short one will do. 3. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out. 4. Never use the passive where you can use the active. 5. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent. 6. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous." — [Orwell, Politics and the English Language](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)
- Orwell's "operators or verbal false limbs" are verb phrases that replace a simple verb with a noun or adjective attached to a general verb (e.g. "render", "serve"), along with passive voice, noun constructions instead of gerunds, and padded prepositional phrases. He also names dying metaphors, pretentious (Latinate) diction, and "meaningless words" with no concrete referent. — [Orwell](https://www.orwellfoundation.com/the-orwell-foundation/orwell/essays-and-other-works/politics-and-the-english-language/)
- Strunk's sentence-level rules: "Use the active voice"; "Put statements in positive form"; "Use definite, specific, concrete language"; "Omit needless words"; "Avoid a succession of loose sentences"; "Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form". — [Strunk](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm)
- GOV.UK: split any sentence over 25 words. Paragraphs have at most 5 sentences. Use active voice (passive is fine when the outcome matters more than the actor). Address the user as "you". Use plain words: "buy" not "purchase", "help" not "assist", "about" not "approximately". Avoid buzzwords and jargon. Words ending in "–ion" and "–ment" often make sentences longer. Explain specialist terms the first time. Use contractions but avoid negative ones ("can't", "don't"). In one study of legal documents, 80% of people preferred clear sentences, and the preference grew with the complexity of the issue. — [GOV.UK clear language](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)
- Google developer docs: "Use active voice"; "Use second person" ("you" rather than "we"); "Put conditions before instructions"; "Be conversational and friendly"; "Don't pre-announce anything"; sentence-case headings; descriptive link text. — [Google style highlights](https://developers.google.com/style/highlights)
- Microsoft top tips (via summaries): "Use bigger ideas, fewer words", "Write like you speak", "Get to the point fast". Start statements with a verb most of the time, and edit out "you can" and "there is/there are/there were". — [summary of MS style guide](https://vickyhella.github.io/docs/writing-styles/ms-style-guide); [Microsoft brand voice](https://learn.microsoft.com/fr-fr/style-guide/brand-voice-above-all-simple-human)
- Disagreement on sentence length: Gopen & Swan reject the word-limit concept ("In place of the word-limit concept, we offer the following definition: A sentence is too long when it has more viable candidates for stress positions than there are stress positions available"), while GOV.UK sets a hard 25-word cap. — [Gopen & Swan](https://www.usenix.org/sites/default/files/gopen_and_swan_science_of_scientific_writing.pdf); [GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/clear-language/)
- Williams: nominalizations hide characters and actions (see §2). — [sobrief](https://sobrief-staging.onrender.com/books/style-2)

### Inferences (machine-observable correlates)
- Sentence length in tokens, excluding punctuation: flag over 25 words (GOV.UK). Also flag more than 2 coordinated or semicolon-joined independent clauses as a proxy for Gopen & Swan's "too many stress candidates".
- Passive voice: spaCy `nsubjpass`/`auxpass` (or `nsubj:pass` and `aux:pass` in UD models). Report a rate rather than flagging every instance, since GOV.UK and Gopen & Swan both allow passives that keep the topic constant. A smart rule would *exempt* passives whose subject continues the previous sentence's topic, because that is exactly Gopen & Swan's "pollen" case.
- Expletive openers: sentences starting "There is/are/was/were" or "It is … that" (Microsoft).
- Nominalization density: tokens with suffixes -tion/-sion/-ment/-ance/-ence/-ity/-ness that have a verb or adjective derivationally related form, per 100 words (GOV.UK, Williams, Orwell). Light-verb + nominalization constructions (Orwell's "operators").
- Word-swap lists: GOV.UK's plain-word substitutions (purchase→buy, assist→help, approximately→about) are directly matchable lemma lists.
- Second person: rate of "you/your" versus "we/the user/users" in instructional text (Google, GOV.UK).
- Stale figures and clichés (Orwell rule 1): phrase-list matching.
- Negative contractions and negative forms (Strunk's "positive form", GOV.UK): count "not"/"n't" constructions.

### Gaps
- The plainlanguage.gov Federal Plain Language Guidelines now redirect to digital.gov, and the landing page fetched had no concrete rules. I did not retrieve the guideline text (e.g. its average-sentence-length and "use 'must'" guidance).
- The Economist style guide was not retrieved.
- Microsoft's official Top 10 page was not fetched directly. The wording comes from secondary summaries.
- No empirical comprehension data was retrieved for the active-voice or short-sentence rules beyond GOV.UK's 80% preference figure.

## 5. Transitions and connectives: useful versus padding

### Takeaway
The evidence is mixed. Explicit connectives speed up online processing, but in Sanders & Noordman (2000) they did *not* improve recall, while the type of coherence relation did. Their benefit is greater for low-knowledge readers. Pinker says to use "the right number" of connectives, since too few leave readers puzzled and too many impose connections where none exist. Purdue recommends transitions at paragraph ends. In essay-scoring studies, connective incidence does not predict quality (§6). For a linter, the defensible prescription is to judge connectives by fit and density rather than reward more of them.

### Cited Findings
- Sanders & Noordman (2000), *Discourse Processes* 29(1): in expository text, problem-solution relations were processed faster, verified better and recalled better than list relations. "Explicit marking of the relations resulted in faster processing but did not affect recall." **[empirical]** — [Tilburg repository abstract](https://repository.tilburguniversity.edu/items/35f9dadc-ca9c-4904-b962-3fcdd68cd1ba)
- Later work reports that connectives help online integration during reading. Offline measures such as free recall and verification have shown less consistent connective effects. **[empirical, secondary]** — [Discourse Processes 2019 article](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/0163853X.2019.1605257)
- Cohesion cues (including causal connectives) help comprehension especially for low-knowledge readers. High-knowledge readers can benefit from cohesion gaps because the gaps prompt inferences. **[empirical, reviewed in]** — [McNamara, Crossley & McCarthy 2010](https://glotta.ntua.gr/NLP/NLP-PostGrad/2018/State-Of-The-Art-Creative_Writing/Linguistic_Features_of_Writing_Quality.pdf)
- Pinker: too few connectives may leave readers puzzled about relations between sentences, and too many impose connections where none exist. Commentators quote him calling coherence connectives "the unsung heroes of lucid prose". He warns that excessive signposting bores and confuses. **[opinion]** — [Journal of Academic Writing review (core.ac.uk)](https://core.ac.uk/download/229873584.pdf); [Geoff Hart review](https://geoff-hart.com/articles/2016/sense-of-style.html)
- Purdue: "Transitions can be used at the end of most paragraphs to help the paragraphs flow one into the next". It lists transition words as one "verbal bridge", alongside repeated key words, synonyms, pronouns and parallel structure. **[opinion]** — [Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/academic_writing/paragraphs_and_paragraphing/index.html)
- Strunk allows single-sentence paragraphs only as "sentences of transition". — [Strunk](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37134/37134-h/37134-h.htm)

### Inferences (machine-observable correlates)
- **Padding connectives:** flag sentence-initial additive or emphatic connectives ("Moreover", "Furthermore", "Additionally", "Importantly", "Notably", "Indeed", "In addition") when density is high. A heuristic is more than 1 per 3 sentences, or two consecutive sentences opening with additive connectives. Flag them also when the connected sentence already shares an entity with the previous one, so the referential link makes the connective redundant. Pinker's "too many impose connections" is the principle behind this.
- **Mismatched connectives:** a contrastive connective ("However", "But", "Yet") with no negation, antonym or contrasting entity across the two sentences. A causal connective ("Therefore", "Thus") is suspicious when the prior sentence has no claim-like content. Detecting this needs semantic checks, so keep it low-confidence.
- **Useful connectives:** causal and adversative connectives that bridge sentences with no lexical overlap (otherwise a "logical gap", per Gopen & Swan) are what the literature supports. Do not flag these.
- Configuration should note that the evidence is for faster processing, not better retention, and that over-explicitness can patronise expert readers.

### Gaps
- No study was retrieved that measures the effect of *additive* connective overuse (the "Moreover/Furthermore" pattern) on reader judgments of quality.
- Degand & Sanders (2002) and Millis & Just (1994) on causal connectives were not retrieved.

## 6. Measurable correlates: Coh-Metrix, TAACO, entity grid, Centering, and judged quality

### Takeaway
*Local* cohesion counts (adjacent-sentence word overlap, connective incidence, coreference) do not predict human essay-quality ratings for skilled L1 writers. Some later studies find them negatively related. *Global* cohesion (overlap between paragraphs) is positively related to quality. Lexical sophistication and syntactic complexity predict ratings better than cohesion does. The entity grid (Barzilay & Lapata 2008), grounded in Centering, models local coherence well for ordering tasks. It helps with essays only when combined with other features. For the linter, this argues against rewarding raw repetition or connective counts. Global topical consistency (paragraph-to-paragraph and heading-to-body overlap) and focus continuity are the better-supported targets.

### Cited Findings
- McNamara, Crossley & McCarthy (2010), *Written Communication* 27(1): 120 argumentative essays by college students, rated by 5 trained raters (inter-rater r ≥ .80). "Using 26 validated indices of cohesion from Coh-Metrix, none showed differences between high- and low-proficiency essays and no indices of cohesion correlated with essay ratings." The best predictors were syntactic complexity (words before main verb, r = .34), lexical diversity (MTLD, r = .20) and word frequency (CELEX, r = –.35). Together these "account for 22% of the variance" (r² = .22, adjusted .20). The authors conclude that "the textual features that characterize good student writing are not aligned with those features that facilitate reading comprehension." **[empirical]** — [McNamara, Crossley & McCarthy 2010 (PDF)](https://glotta.ntua.gr/NLP/NLP-PostGrad/2018/State-Of-The-Art-Creative_Writing/Linguistic_Features_of_Writing_Quality.pdf)
  - Note: one secondary source reports 6% variance for this model. The primary text says 22%. — (search-result discrepancy; primary verified)
- The same paper distinguishes *cohesion* (cues in the text) from *coherence* (the quality of the reader's mental representation). Higher-knowledge readers can build a coherent representation from low-cohesion text. — [McNamara et al. 2010](https://glotta.ntua.gr/NLP/NLP-PostGrad/2018/State-Of-The-Art-Creative_Writing/Linguistic_Features_of_Writing_Quality.pdf)
- Earlier evidence of a link between cohesion and quality came mostly from L2 writing. Liu & Braine (2005) found a moderate relation between referential cohesion and quality for 50 Chinese university students, but there is contrary L2 evidence too. — [McNamara et al. 2010](https://glotta.ntua.gr/NLP/NLP-PostGrad/2018/State-Of-The-Art-Creative_Writing/Linguistic_Features_of_Writing_Quality.pdf)
- Later reviews: "local cohesion indices are generally negatively associated with essay quality (Crossley & McNamara, 2010, 2011) while global cohesion indices are generally positively correlated with essay quality". Paragraph-overlap (global) indices were "strongly predictive". **[empirical, secondary]** — [eScholarship paper citing Crossley & McNamara](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5zv9m93j/qt5zv9m93j.pdf?t=sgibwg)
- Crossley & McNamara (2010), as cited secondarily: human judgments of coherence correlated strongly with judgments of essay quality (r = .80). Perceived coherence matters even though cohesion counts do not track it. **[empirical, secondary]** — [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/content/qt5zv9m93j/qt5zv9m93j.pdf?t=sgibwg)
- TAACO (Tool for the Automatic Analysis of Cohesion) offers over 150 indices, including local (sentence-to-sentence) and global (paragraph-to-paragraph) lexical overlap, synonym overlap, LSA/word2vec similarity and connective counts. Follow-up work using its materials found that lexical overlap between paragraphs strongly predicted essay scores. — [Crossley, Kyle & McNamara, TAACO (Behavior Research Methods)](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13428-015-0651-7); [TAACO 2.0](https://typeset.io/pdf/the-tool-for-the-automatic-analysis-of-cohesion-2-0-47e0vkavev.pdf)
- Barzilay & Lapata (2008), *Computational Linguistics*: an entity-grid model, motivated by Centering Theory, represents a text as a matrix of entities by sentences. Each cell records the entity's grammatical role (Subject, Object, Other, or absent), and coherence is learned from the distribution of role transitions. They evaluated it on sentence ordering, summary coherence rating and readability assessment, mainly with news text. **[empirical/computational]** — [Barzilay & Lapata 2008 (ACL Anthology)](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/J08-1001.pdf); [Lapata entity-grid page](https://homepages.inf.ed.ac.uk/mlap/Papers/egrid.html)
- Burstein, Tetreault & Andreyev (NAACL 2010, ETS) applied the entity grid to student essays. Coherence was best modelled by combining entity-grid features with other writing-quality features. Entity features alone did less well. **[empirical]** — [Burstein et al. 2010](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/u/tetreaul/BTA-naacl10-final-submission.pdf)
- Centering's Rule 2 ordering (CONTINUE > RETAIN > SMOOTH-SHIFT > ROUGH-SHIFT) is a processing-ease claim that is debated (Kibble 2001). — [Kibble 2001](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/J01-4007.pdf)

### Inferences (machine-observable correlates)
- **Do not reward raw local overlap.** High adjacent-sentence repetition is not a quality signal for competent writers and may be a negative one, for example repetitive, low-diversity text. A linter should flag *missing* links (zero overlap, no pronoun, no connective) rather than score more overlap as better. It may also flag *excessive* verbatim repetition, such as a low MTLD or the same noun chunk in 3+ consecutive sentences without pronominalization.
- **Global cohesion is the better-supported rule:** compute content-lemma or embedding overlap between each paragraph and (a) its neighbours, (b) the document title or introduction, and (c) its section heading. Outliers are digression candidates (§3).
- **Entity-grid-lite with spaCy:** build an entity × sentence grid from noun-chunk head lemmas (with pronoun resolution if a coreference component is available, otherwise treat 3rd-person pronouns as continuing the prior subject). Record the role (S = `nsubj`/`nsubjpass`, O = `dobj`/`obj`, X = other, – = absent). Useful features: proportion of S→S transitions (focus continuity), proportion of sentences whose entities are all new (focus breaks), and entities that jump from absent straight to subject with no introduction (new info in topic position, violating Gopen & Swan).
- **Lexical diversity and words-before-main-verb** are the strongest Coh-Metrix predictors of quality ratings. Words before the main verb doubles as Gopen & Swan's subject–verb-separation rule, but note the direction: *more* words before the main verb correlated with *higher* essay ratings (r = .34). Essay raters reward syntactic sophistication, which conflicts with reading-ease advice. A clarity linter should say openly which target it optimises (reader ease rather than rater-perceived sophistication).
- **Connective incidence** should not be scored as "more is better". McNamara et al. found no correlation between cohesion indices (including connectives) and quality.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the primary 2016 Crossley, Kyle & McNamara paper on global versus local cohesion. The global-positive/local-negative pattern comes from secondary citation.
- No retrieved study validates Centering transition ratios against human judgments of quality for expository prose.
- Coh-Metrix "situation model" indices (causal, temporal, intentional cohesion) and LSA givenness were not examined in this pass.
- Entity-grid accuracy figures (e.g. ordering discrimination percentages) were not extracted from the primary paper.
